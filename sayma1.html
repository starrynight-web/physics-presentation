Understanding the Difference Between Fresnel and Fraunhofer Diffraction

Introduction to Diffraction

Diffraction is a wave phenomenon that occurs when a wave encounters an obstacle or aperture. The behavior of diffracted waves depends on the relative distances and sizes involved, leading to two primary regimes: Fresnel (near-field) and Fraunhofer (far-field) diffraction.

The key parameter that determines the diffraction regime is the Fresnel Number:

\[ F = \frac{a^2}{L\lambda} \]

Where \(a\) is the aperture size, \(L\) is the distance to the screen, and \(\lambda\) is the wavelength of light.

  • Fresnel Diffraction (\(F \gg 1\)): Occurs when the observation screen is close to the aperture, and wavefront curvature is significant.
  • Fraunhofer Diffraction (\(F \ll 1\)): Occurs when the observation screen is far from the aperture, and wavefronts can be approximated as planar.

Fresnel Diffraction

Near-Field Diffraction

  • Observation: Screen at finite distance
  • Wavefronts: Spherical or cylindrical curvature
  • Pattern: Changes shape and size with distance
  • Complexity: Richer, more complex patterns
  • Mathematics: Fresnel integrals, convolution
  • Example: Poisson's spot behind circular obstacle

Fraunhofer Diffraction

Far-Field Diffraction

  • Observation: Effectively infinite distance
  • Wavefronts: Planar (flat) wavefronts
  • Pattern: Shape constant, only scales in size
  • Complexity: Simpler, more regular patterns
  • Mathematics: Fourier transform of aperture
  • Example: Laser diffraction through slits

Mathematical Foundation

Both Fresnel and Fraunhofer diffraction originate from the Huygens-Fresnel principle, where every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets.

Fresnel Diffraction Integral

The field at a point is given by:

\[ U(x,y) = \frac{1}{i\lambda} \iint U_0(x',y') \frac{e^{ikr}}{r} dx' dy' \]

Using the Fresnel approximation (\(r \approx z + \frac{(x-x')^2 + (y-y')^2}{2z}\)):

\[ U(x,y) = \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} \iint U_0(x',y') e^{\frac{ik}{2z}[(x-x')^2+(y-y')^2]} dx' dy' \]

Fraunhofer Approximation

When \(z \gg \frac{k(x'^2 + y'^2)_{max}}{2}\), the quadratic phase factor becomes negligible:

\[ U(x,y) = \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} e^{\frac{ik}{2z}(x^2+y^2)} \iint U_0(x',y') e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\lambda z}(xx'+yy')} dx' dy' \]

This shows that the Fraunhofer diffraction pattern is the Fourier transform of the aperture function.

Practical Examples

🔦

Poisson's Spot

A brilliant example of Fresnel diffraction where a bright spot appears in the center of the shadow of a circular obstacle, confirming the wave theory of light.

💿

CD/DVD Diffraction

The rainbow patterns seen on CDs and DVDs are examples of Fraunhofer diffraction, where the closely spaced tracks act as a diffraction grating.

🔍

Laser Diffraction

When a laser beam passes through a small aperture or slit, the resulting pattern on a distant screen demonstrates Fraunhofer diffraction.
